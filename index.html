★ The ½ origin shift

One shared real axis, read with two origins:

  • 0 origin of the number line (primes, counting)
  • ½ origin of the ζ-value frame (the critical line)

ζ is drawn at (½ + Re ζ, t, Im ζ): its real part is measured from the critical line, its imaginary part lives on the depth (Z) axis.

Payoff: a non-trivial zero becomes a geometric incidence — the magenta ribbon touches the critical line exactly when ζ = 0. Zeros are simply where the value-ribbon returns to the line.

Try it: drag “σ” across the strip. One slider moves both halves together — the line being sampled, ζ(σ + it), and the output frame (XZ floor, YZ plane and its axis) that is registered onto it, which slides as one rigid unit. Everything special turns green at ½, and only at ½ does the ribbon actually return to the line.

Read this before drawing conclusions. The intersection is not a new property of ζ uncovered by this picture. The coordinate system is deliberately chosen so that ζ = 0 maps onto the critical line: plotting the real part as ½ + Re ζ forces Re ζ = 0 to land at x = ½, by construction. That is a coordinate registration — a representational choice, not a theorem. Its value is that it makes an existing analytic fact perceptible; it proves nothing on its own.

σ — sweeping across the strip

The σ slider sets Re(s), so the ribbon becomes ζ(σ + it) for any vertical line in the strip, not just the critical one — and carries the output frame's origin along with it, so the origin always sits on the line being sampled.

Why it matters: at σ = ½ the ribbon repeatedly returns to the line — those returns are the zeros. Move σ off ½ and the returns stop: the ribbon generically misses. That is what the Riemann Hypothesis asserts — that every non-trivial zero happens on this one line and nowhere else in the strip.

This shows what an off-line zero would look like; it is not evidence that none exist. Sampling a line can only ever fail to find one.

Watching π(x) emerge from the zeros

Turn on “π(x) from zeros” and press “Animate zeros”. The count sweeps 0 → 50 and the yellow curve is rebuilt at each step.

At N = 0 you get the smooth main term R(x) alone — already a good fit, but featureless. Each zero added contributes one oscillation, and the curve progressively grows the steps of the actual staircase.

The label reports two error measures, and they disagree on purpose:

  • rms — falls steadily as zeros are added (≈ 0.45 → 0.30 over the 50 zeros). This is the sense in which the approximation really is improving.
  • peak — stays near 1 no matter how many zeros you add. π(x) jumps by a whole unit at each prime, and a truncated sum of smooth oscillations always overshoots a jump by a fixed fraction of its height: a Gibbs phenomenon. Convergence here is not uniform, and no number of zeros fixes the spikes at the primes.

Watching those two numbers move in different directions is the honest version of “the primes emerge from the zeros”.

This is a finite-zero approximation to Riemann's explicit formula, not the complete identity — see the README. It converges to π(x) only as N → ∞; at any finite N the leftover oscillation is expected, not a bug.

● Sweeping the XZ plane

Drag “XZ Grid Y” to raise or lower the floor to a height t = Im(s) on the critical line.

The red marker rides the magenta ribbon and reads off ζ(½ + it) at that height — the label shows the value as a + bi together with |ζ| and arg ζ.

A radial line runs from the marker straight to the output-frame axis. It is the value drawn as a phasor: its length is |ζ| and its direction is arg ζ. Sweep t and it rotates and pulses.

The faint phasor trail is the value curve over a window of t flattened into the floor — a flat read on the ribbon's depth (its winding) where perspective is hard. Toggle it with “Show phasor trail” and size it with “trail ± window”.

Whenever the radial line shrinks to nothing — the marker touching the axis, |ζ| → 0 — you are sitting on a non-trivial zero. At t = 0 the marker reads the real value ζ(½) ≈ −1.46.

The three grids
XY plane (Z = 0)
The complex s-plane and the counting plane. X = Re(s), Y = Im(s) = t (also the π-count height). Holds the primes, the staircase, and the zeros on the critical line.
XZ plane (movable floor)
The output-value plane at height t = “XZ Grid Y”. X = Re(ζ), Z = Im(ζ), anchored on the critical line. The red marker reads off ζ at that height.
YZ plane (output-frame origin)
The origin plane of the ζ-value frame. Its grey vertical centre line is the output-frame t-axis — the foot the radial line drops onto. It slides with “Origin shift”; at ½ it sits on the critical line, at 0 on the imaginary axis (Re = 0).
